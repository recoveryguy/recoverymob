Cryotherapy

Whole-body cryotherapy delivers an extreme cold stimulus in two to three minutes through cold air rather than water — a different profile to immersion.

How whole-body cryotherapy works

Whole-body cryotherapy uses refrigerated air or liquid-nitrogen vapour to drop the ambient temperature around you to between −110°C and −140°C. You enter the chamber for two to three minutes wearing minimal clothing, gloves and footwear to protect the extremities, while the rest of the skin surface is exposed to the extreme cold. The skin temperature falls rapidly, but because the exposure is brief and the medium is dry air rather than water, core temperature barely moves over the session. The result is an intense, simultaneous activation of cold receptors across nearly the entire body surface, producing a powerful systemic response in a fraction of the time required by other cold modalities.

The immediate effects mirror other forms of cold exposure but at a different intensity and distribution. Vasoconstriction is rapid and widespread, the sympathetic nervous system fires sharply, and noradrenaline releases throughout the system. Much of the therapeutic value, however, comes from the rewarming phase that follows. As you exit and the body restores normal skin temperature, vasodilation drives a wave of circulation back into the cooled tissue, and a systemic anti-inflammatory shift continues for hours afterward.

The systemic anti-inflammatory effect

The defining feature of whole-body cryotherapy is the breadth of its effect. Where an ice pack cools one joint and an ice bath cools whatever is submerged, cryotherapy delivers a near-total-body cold stimulus that produces a genuinely systemic response. Research has documented downregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines alongside upregulation of anti-inflammatory mediators following whole-body cryotherapy sessions — a shift in the body's overall inflammatory profile rather than a localised cooling effect.

For athletes managing the chronic, low-grade inflammation that comes with sustained high training volume, or managing inflammation across multiple sites at once, this whole-body shift is the main attraction. It is why cryotherapy has been a fixture in professional sport recovery suites for well over a decade, and why athletes with congested competition schedules gravitate toward it — it addresses systemic fatigue and inflammation in a single short session rather than requiring separate treatment of individual areas.

Cryotherapy versus ice baths

Both modalities activate the same fundamental cold pathways, but their profiles differ in ways that make them complementary rather than interchangeable. Water immersion cools muscle tissue more deeply, because water conducts heat far more efficiently than air, and it adds hydrostatic pressure that assists venous and lymphatic return. Cryotherapy delivers a more uniform whole-body surface stimulus in a shorter, dry session that many people find significantly easier to tolerate than sitting in cold water.

Many athletes use both strategically: cryotherapy when time is short or when systemic recovery is the priority, and ice baths when they want deeper muscle cooling and the venous-return benefit that water immersion provides. Neither is universally superior; they are different tools that suit different situations.

The noradrenaline and alertness response

Like other forms of deliberate cold exposure, cryotherapy produces a marked noradrenaline release that drives improved alertness, focus and mood in the hours following a session. Because the stimulus is so intense and so widely distributed, many users report this effect as particularly pronounced. The mood and cognitive lift is one of the reasons cryotherapy has spread beyond elite sport into general wellness use — the recovery benefit and the acute psychological boost arrive together in the same short session.

Where cryotherapy fits in a recovery plan

  • A fast systemic cold stimulus when time is limited and a full ice bath is impractical
  • Managing whole-body inflammation across periods of high training load
  • An accessible entry point for people who find full water immersion difficult to tolerate
  • Tournament and competition schedules with compressed recovery windows between events
  • Stacking with compression and red light in a sequenced recovery session

A sensible approach treats cryotherapy as one option within a broader recovery toolkit rather than a complete recovery solution in itself. It excels at delivering a rapid, systemic, low-time-cost cold stimulus, and it sits comfortably alongside the deeper tissue work of ice baths, the lymphatic drainage of compression, and the cellular repair support of red light therapy.

Practical considerations

Cryotherapy requires specialised equipment, so it is necessarily a facility-based modality rather than something most people can replicate at home. Sessions are brief, which makes them easy to fit into a schedule, and the dry cold is generally more tolerable than equivalent water immersion. The extremities are protected during sessions because they are most vulnerable to the extreme temperatures, and session length is deliberately capped at two to three minutes — the extreme cold means the stimulus is delivered quickly, and longer exposure is neither necessary nor safe. As with any intense cold exposure, people with cardiovascular conditions should seek medical clearance first.

The history and adoption in sport

Whole-body cryotherapy originated in Japan in the late 1970s as a treatment for rheumatoid arthritis, where the systemic anti-inflammatory effect offered relief from joint pain and stiffness. From there it spread through European sports medicine, particularly in Germany and Poland, before becoming a standard fixture in elite sport recovery facilities worldwide. That trajectory — from clinical inflammation management to athletic recovery — tells you something about the mechanism: the same systemic dampening of inflammation that helped arthritis patients is what helps an athlete clear the inflammatory load of heavy training.

Today it appears in the recovery suites of professional teams across virtually every major sport, used both for routine recovery between sessions and for managing the accumulated load of dense competition periods. Its adoption has been driven less by any single dramatic study than by the practical experience of recovery staff who value a fast, repeatable, well-tolerated systemic cold stimulus that athletes will actually use consistently.

Setting expectations

Cryotherapy is a strong tool, but it is not magic, and the marketing around it sometimes overstates the case. It will not substitute for sleep, nutrition or sensible training load management — no recovery modality will. What it does reliably is deliver a fast, systemic, well-tolerated cold stimulus that supports inflammation management and produces an acute lift in mood and alertness. Treat it as one effective component of a complete recovery approach, used consistently rather than occasionally, and it earns its place. Expect it to replace the fundamentals and you will be disappointed.

Two minutes at −120°C is the stimulus. Everything that matters happens in the systemic anti-inflammatory response over the following day.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is cryotherapy better than an ice bath?
Neither is strictly better. Ice baths cool muscle tissue more deeply and add hydrostatic pressure; cryotherapy gives a faster, drier whole-body surface stimulus. Many people use both.
How long is a cryotherapy session?
Two to three minutes. The extreme cold means the stimulus is delivered quickly — longer is neither necessary nor safe.
