Sauna Therapy

Regular sauna use is one of the best-evidenced longevity and recovery practices available. The benefits compound with frequency.

More than relaxation

The wellness industry has done sauna a quiet disservice by selling it primarily as a relaxation experience. It is that — but the physiological effects of regular sauna use run far deeper, and the evidence for them extends well beyond subjective wellbeing into hard cardiovascular, immunological and performance outcomes that accumulate over years of consistent practice.

Heat exposure raises core temperature and drives cardiac output up toward levels seen in moderate aerobic exercise. Heart rate climbs into the 100–150 beat-per-minute range, peripheral vessels dilate, and the body works hard to shed heat through the skin. For people who cannot or do not exercise at sufficient intensity, this represents a genuine cardiovascular training stimulus in its own right. the Kuopio cardiovascular cohort study followed thousands of Finnish men for two decades and found that those using a sauna four to seven times per week had roughly a 40% lower risk of fatal cardiovascular events than once-weekly users — a larger effect than most pharmaceutical interventions achieve, and one that scaled clearly with frequency.

Heat shock proteins and muscle repair

Above roughly 38.5°C core temperature, the body produces heat shock proteins — molecular chaperones that assist in protein folding, prevent protein aggregation under stress, and directly support the repair of exercise-induced muscle damage. studies on heat shock protein expression documents their role in recovery. These proteins are the mechanism by which heat exposure contributes specifically to muscle recovery rather than simply providing comfort and a pleasant sense of calm.

Heat shock protein production is dose-dependent on both temperature and duration. A warm room or a brief few minutes does not induce a meaningful response. Genuine heat stress at 80–90°C sustained for fifteen minutes or more reliably triggers expression. This is precisely why temperature integrity matters so much, and why under-temperature sessions that feel pleasant but never raise core temperature fail to deliver the recovery benefits that a properly run sauna provides. As with cold, the stimulus has to be real to produce the adaptation.

Plasma volume and the performance edge

Regular heat exposure expands plasma volume — the fluid component of blood — through the same adaptive pathway that endurance athletes chase via altitude camps. Increased plasma volume improves heat dissipation during exercise, reduces cardiovascular strain at any given workload, and is associated with measurable improvements in aerobic performance. For Australian athletes who routinely compete in summer heat, sauna-based heat acclimation is directly applicable and effectively free, requiring nothing more than consistent post-training sessions in the weeks before competition.

Growth hormone release is another well-documented adaptation. Sauna sessions of sufficient temperature and duration have produced growth hormone increases of up to sixteen-fold in some protocols — a magnitude reflecting the body's anabolic response to the heat stress and the recovery period that follows. For athletes, this adds to the anabolic environment that supports the muscle repair occurring during the recovery window, stacking on top of the adaptations driven by training itself.

Finnish versus infrared

Finnish saunas run 80–95°C and heat the air, often with löyly — water poured on hot stones to produce a burst of steam that briefly intensifies the perceived heat. Infrared saunas run cooler, typically 50–65°C, but emit wavelengths that penetrate tissue directly, heating from within rather than from the air outward. Both produce heat shock proteins and parasympathetic activation. The practical difference is that Finnish saunas deliver a stronger cardiovascular stimulus at higher ambient temperatures, while infrared allows longer, more tolerable sessions with deeper tissue penetration at a gentler air temperature.

Neither is strictly superior. The best choice is the one you will use consistently and can tolerate at a genuinely therapeutic intensity. Access, comfort and schedule matter more than chasing a theoretical optimum that you only manage once a fortnight.

The parasympathetic shift

The deep calm that settles in as body temperature normalises after a sauna is not incidental — it reflects a genuine shift from sympathetic toward parasympathetic nervous system dominance. Cortisol drops. Heart rate variability improves. This is the same nervous-system state in which the body does its recovery work, which is why an evening sauna so reliably improves sleep onset and quality in regular users. The heat raises core temperature; the subsequent cool-down produces a rebound drop that signals the circadian clock toward sleep, accelerating the natural temperature decline that normally accompanies sleep onset.

For athletes carrying significant nervous-system load from high training volume — the chronic sympathetic tone, the difficulty switching off, the disrupted sleep — regular sauna use addresses the root state rather than masking the symptoms. It is one of the most reliable non-pharmacological tools for shifting an over-aroused nervous system back toward recovery.

Sauna and athletic recovery

Used immediately after training, sauna amplifies the inflammatory-resolution process already underway, continues the clearance of metabolic byproducts through sweating, and reduces the residual muscular tension that contributes to next-day soreness. Its most powerful application, though, is as the heat phase of a contrast protocol — sauna first, cold immersion second, repeated in cycles. The heat primes the vasculature and creates the physiological conditions under which the cold's vasoconstriction and anti-inflammatory effects compound rather than simply occurring in isolation.

Protocol

  • 80–90°C for Finnish, or 55–65°C for infrared
  • 15–25 minutes per round, ideally entering already warmed up from training
  • A minimum of three sessions per week for cardiovascular adaptation to accumulate
  • Hydrate thoroughly before and after — sauna is significantly dehydrating
  • For contrast, follow with cold immersion at 4–8°C within two to three minutes of exiting
  • For sleep, time the session 90–120 minutes before bed to exploit the temperature-drop rebound

The cardiovascular and longevity benefits are dose-dependent, so frequency is the lever that matters most. Adding a fifteen-to-twenty-minute session onto existing training days, three to five times a week, sits squarely in the range that produced the largest effects in the Finnish cohort data — and pairs naturally with cold for a complete contrast practice.

The cardiovascular benefit is dose-dependent. Four-plus sessions a week is where the Finnish data shows the largest effect.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I use a sauna?
Three to seven sessions per week. The Finnish cohort data shows benefits increasing with frequency, with the largest cardiovascular effect in those using a sauna four or more times weekly.
Is infrared or Finnish sauna better?
Both work. Finnish saunas give a stronger cardiovascular stimulus at higher air temperatures; infrared penetrates tissue at a more tolerable temperature, allowing longer sessions. Choose based on tolerance and access.
How long should a sauna session last?
15–25 minutes per round at therapeutic temperature. Shorter sessions don't reliably trigger heat shock protein expression.
