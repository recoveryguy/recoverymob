Why Athletes Run Chronically Low on Magnesium

Magnesium deficiency in athletes is quiet and common — and it shows up as fatigue, cramps, and poor sleep blamed on other things.

Magnesium deficiency in athletes is quiet, common, and expensive — and almost none of its symptoms get attributed to their actual cause. It rarely announces itself with one dramatic problem. Instead it produces a scattered handful of moderate ones: persistent fatigue that does not lift with rest, sleep that never feels quite deep enough, muscle cramps, more frequent niggles and tweaks, and a performance ceiling that simply will not move no matter how hard you train. Each of these gets blamed on something else — overtraining, stress, age, bad luck — while the underlying mineral deficit goes unaddressed for months or years. a review of magnesium status in athletes finds suboptimal magnesium status common in athletes even among those eating what looks like a good diet.

Why the deficit builds in the first place

The core problem is that athletes lose magnesium faster than non-athletes and need more of it. Sweat carries 3 to 8 milligrams of magnesium per litre. An athlete sweating out one to two litres in a hard session can lose well over ten milligrams of magnesium in that workout alone, and the losses climb in heat and at higher intensities. Repeat that several times a week and the cumulative drain steadily exceeds what the diet puts back. Unlike some nutrients the body stores in large buffers, magnesium balance is tight enough that a sustained daily shortfall shows up as functional deficiency reasonably quickly.

Diet frequently fails to keep pace, and not because athletes are careless. Magnesium is concentrated in dark leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds and dark chocolate — foods many people eat inconsistently. Worse, modern intensive agriculture has progressively depleted magnesium from farmed soils, and studies estimate the magnesium content of common foods has fallen by twenty to thirty percent compared with mid-twentieth-century levels. So even an athlete eating a genuinely sensible diet can be running a persistent magnesium deficit through no fault of their own — the food simply contains less of the mineral than it used to, while their training demands more of it than a sedentary person's would.

What the deficiency costs you

Magnesium is a cofactor in over three hundred enzymatic reactions, which explains why a shortfall produces such a wide and easily-misread spread of symptoms. The most fundamental role concerns energy itself: every molecule of ATP, the body's energy currency, must be bound to a magnesium ion to be usable. When magnesium is low, cellular energy production runs below capacity, felt as hitting a fatigue wall earlier at the same training effort. The athlete tires sooner, assumes they are overtrained or under-recovered, and never suspects a mineral.

Magnesium also controls the calcium-magnesium exchange behind muscle contraction and relaxation. Calcium signals a muscle to contract; magnesium is required for it to relax again afterward. Run low and muscles struggle to fully release, which shows up as cramping — including the nocturnal cramps many athletes simply accept as part of training hard. And magnesium is deeply involved in sleep: it acts as a natural antagonist at the NMDA receptor, putting a brake on the excitatory glutamate signalling that keeps the nervous system aroused. Depleted athletes routinely describe trouble switching off at night and shallow, unrefreshing sleep — a pattern that frequently traces back to magnesium status rather than to stress or screen time.

Fixing it: forms and routes that work

  • Use oral glycinate or malate as the daily foundation — both are well absorbed, and glycinate's mild calming effect makes it ideal in the evening
  • Avoid magnesium oxide, which looks impressive on the label but is poorly absorbed and largely passes through
  • Prioritise magnesium-rich whole foods: leafy greens, nuts, seeds and legumes
  • Add a transdermal soak as an evening top-up that bypasses the gut's absorption ceiling entirely

Oral dosing is genuinely useful but limited — the gut downregulates absorption as stores fill, and higher doses produce a laxative effect that caps how much you can take at once. This is exactly where transdermal delivery earns its place. Magnesium absorbed through the skin during a warm soak sidesteps the gut entirely, and the heat both improves absorption and adds its own parasympathetic, sleep-promoting effect. For Melbourne athletes, recovery services for Melbourne athletes increasingly include heated magnesium soaks as an evening recovery option for precisely this reason: the warm water drives the mineral in while the heat itself helps shift an over-aroused nervous system toward the state that good sleep requires. It addresses the deficit and the sleep disruption in a single session.

How to tell it is working

One frustrating quirk of magnesium is that a standard blood test is a poor guide to your actual status. Blood magnesium is tightly regulated and represents only about one percent of the body's total magnesium, so serum levels can look normal while tissue stores are depleted. This is part of why the deficiency so often goes undetected — the obvious test does not catch it. In practice, the most reliable signal is symptomatic. Within a couple of weeks of genuine repletion, most athletes notice improved sleep quality first, then fewer cramps, then a general sense of recovering more completely between sessions. That subjective improvement, rather than a lab number, is usually what confirms the gap has been closed. Most athletes, frankly, have never been properly magnesium-replete and do not know what it is supposed to feel like — which is exactly why the change, when it comes, tends to be more noticeable than they expected.

How much, and when

The dietary reference intake sits around 400 to 420mg daily for adult men and 310 to 320mg for adult women, but hard-training athletes sit at the upper end of requirement because of their sweat losses, and many need to exceed the baseline figure. A practical approach is to cover as much as possible through magnesium-rich food, then add a supplemental 200 to 400mg of elemental magnesium from a well-absorbed form, dialling the dose back if any loosening of the stool appears as a sign you have hit your absorption ceiling. Timing matters too: glycinate taken in the evening supports sleep, while malate suits the morning if you find magnesium gives you a settled, non-stimulating steadiness through the day. Pairing the daily oral foundation with an occasional transdermal soak after the heaviest training days covers both the chronic baseline need and the acute spike in losses that a hard session produces. None of this is complicated or expensive — it is simply rarely done, which is why magnesium deficiency remains one of the most common and most overlooked limiters of recovery in otherwise well-prepared athletes.

Most athletes have never been properly magnesium-replete and don't know what it feels like. The sleep improvement when levels normalise is the tell.

FAQ

How do athletes lose so much magnesium?
Through sweat — 3 to 8mg per litre. Several hard sessions a week deplete magnesium faster than a typical diet replaces it.
What's the best way to top up magnesium?
Oral glycinate or malate works well; avoid poorly-absorbed oxide. A transdermal magnesium soak in the evening is a useful addition that bypasses the gut's absorption ceiling.
